I knew early in high school that I wanted to be in the driver's seat of my own life. 

Junior Achievement, a workshop that teaches young kids about entrepreneurship, planted that seed. The idea that business wasn't something that happened to other people, it was something you could _build_. 

My plan was straightforward, if a little ambitious: study finance, spend a few years inside a real company to understand how things worked from the inside, then go start something of my own.

What I didn't plan on was a three-year detour before I completed college.

My parents couldn't afford to pay for college. Not a complaint, it's just how the dice came up.

 The problem was that they were still filing their taxes with me listed as a dependent, which effectively disqualified me from financial aid I would otherwise have been eligible for. 
 
 So I waited. 
 
 Three years at Diablo Valley College while the calendar ran out, doing what I could, until I aged out of their return and became, at least in the eyes of the IRS, my own financial entity.

The moment that changed, the doors to real funding opened, and I transferred to Saint Mary's College in Moraga, California.

I enrolled in the Honors Finance program pretty much right out of the gate.  Selective (for a college with ~2k students in it), demanding, and led by a professor named John Thompson, a retired CEO of a Chicago bank who had the particular gift of people who've actually done things: he taught finance the way someone teaches you to drive a car rather than draw one. I graduated with a B.S. in Finance in 1990.

---

**Paying for it was entirely up to me.**

Since financial aid covered only so much, I worked — constantly, creatively, and in whatever form allowed me to study while on the clock.

During my junior year, I pulled 16-hour shifts for AAA road service. There's a specific kind of clarity that comes from being alone on a highway at 2am, and I used every quiet stretch to read. I bussed tables at a restaurant. I spent time helping someone build a house in Inverness — just the two of us, constructing a multi-level coastal home from the ground up.

That job surprised me. 

Construction turns out to involve a significant amount of math, and I came away with a respect for the physical logic of building things that I've never quite lost.

But the work that shaped me most during those years was the kind I stumbled into almost by accident.

---

A close friend of my worked as an admin for a legal services company and she told me they needed independent contractors. Specifically, someone who was available, mobile, and reliable.

I was all three.

The word "independent" in the job title sang to me like a songbird, because life sucked particularly hard sitting in a classroom followed by more sitting in an office. Plus, I could read paper maps like a demon thanks to my experience working for AAA road service. So wherever they needed me to go, I could damn well find it, not sitting in an office.

I didn't even care, at the time, that the job entailed delivering civil court subpeonas to people who were being sued. 

If I would have known that I would ultimately face a gun pointed at my face three times, threatened battery many other times, and otherwise ruin the day of perfectly fine people, I may have declined the offer.

But, I didn't. And that one job led to a relationship with a second legal services company, which led to a third. The work started to snowball in that quiet, word-of-mouth way that happens when you show up and do what you say you'll do.

The third relationship is the one I remember most vividly, for reasons that have nothing to do with the work itself. The man who ran it had a small, cluttered office. Every time he briefed me on a job, he'd sit me down, pull the filter clean off his cigarette, and smoke the whole thing unfiltered while he talked. I don't know if it was affectation or habit, but it had the effect of making every assignment feel slightly more cinematic than it probably was.

That's the job where subpoena delivery turned into surveillance work.

He put me on a stakeout, actual private investigative fieldwork, which led to further stakeout jobs. They were perfect. I would sit in my car and study for exams while long stretches of nothing happened and get paid for it.

I could write a separate article about what happened when the following did happen. It was usually on behalf of a divorcee who wanted to know where her ex is pulling money out of a bank that he didn't tell her lawyers about. It wasn't interesting noir stuff, but it's interesting to talk about now.

From there, the territory expanded further into something called heir tracing: the process of locating individuals who are entitled to a portion of an estate, often without knowing it. It was an unusual corner of the legal world to find yourself in as a college student, and I mention it less as a credential than as a marker of how strange and interesting the path was.
 
I solved one right at the end of college and spent it crossing Europe with my friends who had parent money.

---

**What I actually learned.**

By the time I graduated, I had a finance degree and something harder to put on paper: I knew how to build a client base from nothing. My legal services work was never formal — no business cards, no office, no LLC. It was a network I cultivated through reliability and referrals, client by client, until I had something that functioned like a freelance practice. I had learned, essentially, how to sell trust.

That turned out to be more useful than almost anything else.
