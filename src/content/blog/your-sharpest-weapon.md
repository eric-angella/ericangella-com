You're probably not wrong about everything, only some things.

But those *some things* can turn into big problems if you don't watch it.

I'm not talking about basic facts, like someone's birthday, or how old your best friend is. These are small things where your recall is either right or wrong. When you say them out loud in the presence of someone who has it right, like the person with the birthday, you might get embarrassed and sometimes corrected, and that's about it.

It's the bigger things that make up your core beliefs that count. They'll bite you in the long run if you start drifting off without some humility checks. And the longer they run off course, the harder they eventually bite.

Think of your beliefs as software, version 1.0. You built it fast, not knowing what you don't know, coding away because you just want to ship it. It isn't perfect, but that's by design. Perfect software isn't expected on version 1.0. That's what patches and version 1.1 are for.

The problem is most of us stopped patching years ago.

---
## Dead reckoning

Wrongish doesn't announce itself.

It also feeds on dead air and still manages to grow into something bigger and meaner over time.

Pre-tech sailors navigated by dead reckoning. To estimate their position, they would start from a known point and use speed, heading and time to rough it out. Small errors were deadly.

A single degree of error is invisible at first. Comfortable, even. But across an ocean, that one degree will land you on the wrong continent.

Most bad beliefs work the same way. They're not catastrophically off, just by a degree or two. And that's exactly what makes them dangerous. You never feel lost until you are. 

---
## Personal experience

I held a belief for years, throughout college, that confidence was the same thing as competence. I performed certainty like it was a credential. 

It worked, or so it seemed, until it didn't.

My first year in corporate life handled that. 

I was a management trainee at a mutual fund. Me and my fellow MT's (affectionately known as "minute takers") handled ourselves, with the help of a few smoke-blowing masters above us, that we were god's gift and creation.

Over the next two years of the program, we found ourselves in rooms where real competence was visible and our version 1.0 confidence had zero underneath it.

The ones that adapted in real time, that dug in and built some competence to match their outward confidence thrived big time.

---
## Embrace just one paradox

The fix is simple, but the execution is hard for some people.

Just hold one paradox in your head:

The people most committed to assuming that they're wrong as a default assumption are the ones who end up the most right.

Don't confuse this with self-doubt. It's not. And you're not weak or stuck in a state of second-guessing. It's committing to a *system of self-correction*.

And it's the only posture that keeps the software current.

So the goal isn't humility as a personality trait. It's versioning as a practice. Patch your beliefs the way good engineers patch code. Regularly, based on new data, without ego attached to the previous release.

You won't get to right. But you can get to less wrong than yesterday.

That's the only direction that matters.
