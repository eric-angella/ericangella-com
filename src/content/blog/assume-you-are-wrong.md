---
title: "Assume You Are Wrong About Everything"
excerpt: "You'll be a much smarter person"
date: 2026-08-07
---
You're probably not wrong about everything. But you're wrongish. And wrongish, held long enough, compounded across enough decisions, lands you somewhere you never intended to be.

Think of your beliefs as software. Version 1.0 — built fast, on incomplete data, by a younger version of you who was mostly just trying to ship. Most software that launches is imperfect by design. The difference between good software and abandoned software isn't whether bugs exist. It's whether anyone bothered to patch them.

The problem is most of us stopped patching years ago.

Here's the thing about wrongish: it doesn't announce itself. Old sailors navigated by dead reckoning — estimating position from a known point, using speed and heading and time. A single degree of error is invisible at the start. Comfortable, even. But sustained across an ocean, one degree puts you on the wrong continent. Most bad beliefs work the same way. They're not catastrophically off. They're just a degree or two from true — and that's exactly what makes them dangerous. You never feel lost until you are.

I held a belief for years that confidence was the same thing as competence. I performed certainty like it was a credential. It worked, until it didn't — until I was in rooms where real competence was visible and my version 1.0 confidence had nothing underneath it. The update was uncomfortable. The update was necessary.

Here's the paradox: the people most committed to being wrong are the ones who end up most right. Assuming wrongness isn't self-doubt. It's not a posture of weakness or permanent second-guessing. It's a system. It's the only posture that keeps the software current.

So the goal isn't humility as a personality trait. It's versioning as a practice. Patch your beliefs the way good engineers patch code — regularly, based on new data, without ego attached to the previous release.

You won't get to right. But you can get to less wrong than yesterday.

That's the only direction that matters.
