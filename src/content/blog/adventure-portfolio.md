---
title: Adventure Portfolio
excerpt: "Take the adventure"
date: 2026-08-07
---
My older daughter is 14. She has a summer to enjoy before she's off to high school. She seems more enthused than intimidated, which is good.

Like most parents, I feel a deep urge to tell her how careers work. How you think about them, how you build them block by block. Practical ways to point your future in the right direction and away from the wrong one.

The hardest part is getting them to think you know anything. Because they grew up _just now_, in an age where things look so different that none of the old advice seems to apply anymore.

In some ways they're right. If I was to tee up a lecture about how things work, it might look like this: Pick a direction. Get the degree. Find the job. Move up.

On the surface, that is kind of how it works. You need to go to college. You need to pick a degree. The degree points to the job, and the job points to the next one. It's a track worn smooth by generations of well-meaning parents.

But increasingly, that advice is a map for a territory that isn't there anymore.

---

I spent seven years at Franklin Templeton, starting with their management training program. I learned finance, leadership, investment, and how to run a project. I figured those were the basics needed to eventually run your own thing.

And eventually I did. I quit and spent years inside music and internet startups until I started my own, with a partner, called Groove Engine. I raised a seed round. I hired people. I watched things not go according to plan and adapted anyway.

After that I spent years tech consulting with a small remote team. We designed websites, built iPhone apps, serviced clients, delivered whatever technology they needed. I let others dig for gold. I just sold them the pickaxe.

At the time, I didn't think of it as building a portfolio. With each pivot, I thought I was just moving on to the next logical thing.

Now I ghostwrite for technology executives. It's the most lucrative, rewarding, and focused work I've ever done. And it's not a job I found, it's a job I became capable of through accumulation. The finance background helps me frame technology investment decisions. The startup years taught me to move fast, raise money, and stay alive. The consulting years taught me how to service clients and build anything.

None of those tracks were a plan. Together, they're irreplaceable.

You can't replicate that stack by following a ladder.

---

The traditional career track is a staircase. Each step is defined. The path from analyst to manager to director is well-lit and well-traveled. You know what's expected. You know what comes next.

That model still exists. It's just no longer the only viable one. And increasingly, it's not the most valuable one.

The market is starting to reward people who have accumulated across multiple domains. People who can sit at the intersection of finance and technology, or operations and storytelling, or design and data. These aren't generalists. They're people with a specific combination of skills that the market wants and almost no one else has.

That's an adventure portfolio. And unlike the staircase, you can start building it at 14.

---

My daughter doesn't need to know what she wants to be. She needs to start collecting. Pursue the thing she's curious about now, even if it seems unrelated to anything practical. Learn how money works. Build something, even if it's small. Develop a skill someone will pay for. Study how businesses operate.

None of it will look like a career path. All of it will compound.

The staircase tells you what step comes next. The adventure portfolio asks a different question: _what are you adding to your stack?_

She's a moat-builder, not a ladder-climber. I just needed to tell her that.

I wish someone had told me at 22. I'm telling her now, at 14.

She has a head start I never had.
