Keep your identity small.

Great hook for an article that begs you to read on if you care about such things.

It's the name of a short essay published by Paul Graham in 2009. He was inspired to write it after noticing the internet spawned an unprecedented wall of angry noise.

His point was simple and powerful: the moment a belief becomes a part of your identity, you stop reasoning about it and start defending it.

Paul is a bit of a philosopher-legend in the software startup world, and a real piece of history in the valley's formative years, particularly the dotcom era.

In his essay, still published on his Smithsonian-grade website straight out of the late 90s, he wanders through his thought process until the logical end:

*"So keep your identity small, if you want to hold on to your ability to reason."

I like my ability to reason, so it bounced around the back of my mind for years. It also limited my enthusiasm for recreational social media to this day.

## Before the attention machine was built

Paul's essay was written well before the modern attention machine was built. Facebook was only three years old when he wrote it. Mark and his personal nerd army were far from figuring out how to hijack your attention by feeding your rage via algorithm.

But figure it out they did, and here we are suffering through a world that rewards identity performance aimed at pissing you off.

Try it yourself. 

Post something exploratory like a question, a half-formed idea, or a belief you're still working through, and see what happens.

Thud.

If you consider yourself in the *frustrated middle*, those who ignore the news media and wonder why people are so angry all the time, put a pin on it for a second.

Now post something declarative. Thrust a flag into the ground, draw a line, choose a side. Give your readers a reason to get mad and do something about it.

Make it loud. 

Engagement is everything on social media platforms, and nothing engages like loudness.

Embrace your outrage, loyalty, and tribal signaling in a single package. 

The outrage machine is waiting for you.

## Trained monkeys

But here's the problem. 

The algorithm burns these fuels efficiently, and while it does work, it's molded an entire generation of creators, founders, and professionals to perform identity like a trained monkey rather than develop it.

So now we're left with lots of loud people with ever-shrinking credibility.

It's shrinking because loudness has a short half-life, and people are tired of it. It requires constant escalation. Each declaration has to be bigger than the last to capture the same amount of attention, and the audience you build is held together by the performance, not by genuine trust. 

The moment you stop performing, they leave. The moment you change your mind, or worse, admit you were wrong, the identity you built *works against you*.

## Dig, and build

Value, however, compounds.

The person who shows their real thinking outside the algorithmic capture and shares what they've actually learned, helps someone solve a real problem. They're building something different. 

A slower audience, maybe. 

A smaller one, at first. But one that's there because of what you know and how you think, not because of how loudly you declared which team you're on.

Humanity has a chance to thrive if the winners of the next decade aren't the loudest people in the room, but the value-builders.

They'll be the ones who resisted the incentive to perform and did the harder, quieter work of becoming genuinely useful. That path takes longer. It offers fewer dopamine hits along the way. 

But it doesn't require you to defend a version of yourself you've painted into a corner.

Keep your identity small. Put your value out there. 

Let the loud ones have the crack-pipe engagement metrics.
